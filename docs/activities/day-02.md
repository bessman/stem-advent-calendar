# Day 2 · Sink or Float?

**Field:** Physics · Buoyancy · Engineering design  
**Time:** 10–15 minutes (Wednesday 2 December 2026)  
**Age group:** 6–9  
**Progression:** On [Day 1](day-01.md), we practised **predict → test → observe**. Today we use the same method to find out why **shape** can change whether an object floats. This also prepares us to test and redesign structures on Days 5–6. Nothing from today's box needs to be saved.

## Introduction

Why can a heavy ship float while a little lump of clay sinks? Today's challenge is to turn **one piece of modelling clay** into two different shapes and see what changes in water. The clay stays the same: only its shape changes.

By the end of this mission, the child can:

- Predict whether a clay ball or clay boat will float, then test both.
- Describe how changing a shape changes what happens in water.
- Improve a design after observing a result, instead of treating a failed attempt as the end.

**The big question:** Can you make something that sinks **float without changing what it is made of**?

## Adult instructions

### Preparation (2–4 minutes before opening)

1. Buy or use **non-hardening plasticine** that is heavier than water as a solid ball. Test the actual clay in water before the day of the activity. Do not use light/foam clay or dough.
2. Prepare a 20–25 g portion. Check that it can be pressed into a **roughly 6–8 cm wide, thin-bottomed dish with raised edges**, and that this shape can float in a sufficiently wide container. A 6–8 cm boat is made **after opening** and does not need to fit inside the advent box.
3. Place the bowl or deep tray on a stable, wipeable surface. Fill it with enough cool water (about **5–8 cm deep**) for the clay to sink without hitting the bottom immediately. Keep a towel and plate nearby.
4. Put only the clay and folded mission card inside the box.

### How to help (10–15 minutes total)

| Time | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 0–2 min | Recall Day 1's prediction routine. Ask whether the clay ball will sink or float. |
| 2–4 min | Let the child place the ball gently into the water and observe it sinking. |
| 4–8 min | Return **all** the clay to the child to flatten and pinch into a broad, hollow boat. Help only if needed. |
| 8–12 min | Test the boat. If it sinks, help make its base wider, its bottom even and its sides taller; test again. |
| 12–15 min | Compare shapes. Ask: “What stayed the same? What did we change?” Dry the table and hands. |

**Keep it a fair comparison:** Use the **same entire piece** of clay and the **same bowl of water** for each test. The intentionally changed variable is shape. Remind the child to lower the boat onto the water **open side up** and avoid splashing water into it.

### Safety

- **Adult supervision:** Keep water on a stable surface and wipe spills promptly.
- **Do not eat the clay.** Check the manufacturer's age guidance and wash hands after handling. Keep clay away from toddlers and pets.
- Use **cool tap water only**. No hot water, chemicals, electrical devices or mains power are needed.
- Avoid using clay that crumbles or dissolves in water; discard water responsibly and do not wash clay lumps into the drain.

### Why it works

Water pushes upward on an object. This upward force is called **buoyancy**. The more water an object pushes aside (*displaces*), the greater the upward force on it.

The compact clay ball is denser than water, and it cannot displace enough water before becoming completely submerged, so it sinks. When the **same clay** becomes a broad, hollow boat, it encloses air and can displace **more water before its rim goes under**. If the weight of that displaced water is enough to balance the weight of the clay, the boat floats.

It's not simply that “flat things float.” The useful design feature is the **wide hollow shape with raised sides** that keeps water out. If water spills inside, the boat can sink. Real ships use the same principle: their overall shape includes a large amount of air, so their average density is low enough to float.

### Troubleshooting and optional extension

- **The clay ball floats:** The material is too light for this comparison. Try denser, non-hardening plasticine; do not add metal weights to make the demonstration work.
- **The clay boat sinks immediately:** Make a broader, shallower bottom with thinner, even walls and raised edges. Check for holes and let the child lower it gently onto the surface.
- **The boat fills with water:** Pinch cracks closed, make the rim taller and try again in still water.
- **The child struggles to form a boat:** An adult can press the first flat disk; let the child shape the sides and choose the next improvement.
- **Extra challenge for older children:** Try a narrow boat and a wide boat from the same amount of clay. Which is easier to keep afloat? What might explain the difference?
