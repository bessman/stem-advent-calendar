## Introduction

Is a dark marker really just one colour? Draw a small dot on filter paper and let water creep up from below. Some of the marker's pigments travel at different speeds and separate into bands.

By the end of this mission, the child can:

- Make a prediction about whether a dark marker contains one colour or several.
- Observe pigments separating as water moves through paper.
- Explain that chromatography can separate parts of a mixture.

**The big question:** What colours are hiding inside a dark marker?

## Adult instructions

### Preparation (2–3 minutes before opening)

1. Test the chosen washable, water-based marker on a spare coffee-filter strip. Look for at least two pigment colours separating as water rises. Try another marker if the first stays as one colour.
2. Cut two white coffee-filter strips about **2 × 8 cm**. Draw a pencil line about 1 cm from one end if helpful; do not use ink for the line.
3. Set out a small clear cup with a little water, a pencil to suspend the paper, tape and a plate or tray. Pack the strips, marker and card.

### How to help (10–15 minutes total)

| Time | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 0–2 min | Ask whether the dark marker might contain more than one pigment. |
| 2–5 min | Draw a small, dense dot across the strip about 1 cm from the lower end. Rest the pencil across the cup and tape the upper end of the paper to it. |
| 5–10 min | Add water so the paper's bottom edge is in the water but the marker dot stays above it. Watch the water rise through the paper. |
| 10–13 min | When the water has travelled most of the way up, lift out the strip and observe the separated colours. Try the second strip with another marker colour. |
| 13–15 min | Compare the bands and leave the strips on a plate to dry. |

**Keep it a fair comparison:** Keep the paper type, marker dot height and water depth the same when comparing markers. The marker ink must not be submerged directly; otherwise it may wash off into the cup instead of separating on the paper.

### Safety

- Use washable, non-toxic, water-based markers and check the age guidance. Do not use permanent markers or solvents.
- The marker is for paper, not skin. Do not taste the ink or experiment water; wash hands after use.
- Wipe up drips and keep the water away from electronics.

### Why it works

Marker ink is a mixture of coloured pigments. Water is the **mobile phase**: it moves through tiny spaces in the filter paper and carries some pigments with it. The paper is the **stationary phase**. Different pigments dissolve in water and stick to the paper by different amounts, so they travel at different speeds and separate into bands.

This process is called **chromatography**. It is used in laboratories to separate and study mixtures. A single dark-looking ink can contain several colours, while some markers may contain only one pigment that moves visibly in this test.

### Troubleshooting and optional extension

- **The ink washes into the cup:** The dot is below the water line. Start with a fresh strip and keep the dot above the water.
- **No colours separate:** Try a different washable marker. Some inks use pigments that do not separate clearly in water.
- **The water does not climb:** Use absorbent coffee filter paper and make sure its bottom edge touches the water.
- **The strip falls into the cup:** Tape it securely to the pencil and rest the pencil across the cup rim.
- **Extra challenge for older children:** Compare two washable marker colours and measure how far each pigment band travels from the starting dot.
